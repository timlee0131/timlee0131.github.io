## Brief Introduction to Standard Attention

Originally introduced for neural machine translation in 2017, transformers have since been applied to virtually every other area in deep learning with overwhelming success. From natural language processing (GPT, BERT, etc.) to computer vision (ViT), transformers dominate the current state of the art. Underneath all of this success lies the attention mechanism that powers transformers, and thereby the modern AI boom. By dynamically weighting input elements based on their relevance to each token (an atomic unit of data like a word in a sentence), attention mechanisms allow transformers to capture long-range dependencies and contextual relationships within a given context window. This selective focus on information across the entire context window facilitates parallel computation and is provably more powerful than methods like Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) that cannot remember the entire context. The attention layer's ability to model relationships between all pairs of tokens regardless of their distance has proven crucial for transformers' success across natural language processing, computer vision, time series processing, and beyond.

Standard attention in transformers operates through a query-key-value ($Q,K,V$) mechanism implementing scaled dot-products. The process begins by projecting input embeddings $X \in \mathbb{R}^{L\times d}$ into three distinct representations: queries ($Q$), keys ($K$), and values ($V$) through learned linear transformations where $Q, K, V \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d_{model}}$. For each token, relevance scores are computed by taking the dot product between its query vector and all key vectors, capturing the similarity between tokens. This dot product is followed by a softmax non-linearity, converting the scores into a probability distribution. Softmax is used (and not any other non-linearity function like ReLU) for the query-key (QK) attention scores because it normalizes the scores into a probability distribution that sums to 1, ensuring that the weighted aggregation of values remains properly scaled regardless of sequence length. The final output for each position is a weighted sum of value vectors $V$, where weights come from the corresponding softmax probabilities. The denominator $\sqrt{d_k}$ is used to scale the $QK$ product.

$Attention(X)=softmax(QK^T/\sqrt{d_k})V; \space Q = XW_Q, \space K = XW_K, \space V = XW_V$

## Issues with Standard Attention

Despite the remarkable success of the standard attention mechanism across domains, its fundamental limitations have become increasingly apparent in recent years. Ironically, attention mechanism’s biggest limitations are imposed by the very property that makes attention so powerful: each token attending to every other token in the sequence. This incurs a $(L \times L)$ quadratic complexity along the sequence length (i.e. in $QK^T$) and renders processing of long sequences prohibitively expensive, creating bottlenecks for applications requiring extended context. In addition, despite its theoretical capacity to only focus on relevant tokens, attention mechanism’s indiscriminate interactions with all tokens regardless of relevance makes the model susceptible to noise accumulation in practice. These constraints have catalyzed research into attention variants in recent years, tackling complexity bottlenecks and practical performance limitations observed in standard attention. 

The remainder of this article explores attention variants organized into two fundamental but related categories: (1) mechanisms that address computational complexity bottlenecks by reducing the quadratic scaling problem, and (2) approaches that enhance model performance by systematically limiting noise accumulation in attention distributions.

## Attention Variants for Reducing Complexity Overheads

There are mainly two types of attention variants that address the computational complexity present in standard attention: sparse attention and linear attention. 

### Sparse Attention

Sparse attention aims to preserve the standard softmax attention structure but reduce the quadratic bottleneck with induced sparsity. Some of the most seminal works in sparse attention include Reformer [Kitaev, 2020] and BigBird [Zaheer, 2020]. 

Reformer is developed from the perspective that softmax amplifies dot products $QK^T$ between similar tokens anyway, which indicates that always performing the full quadratic attention matrix may not be necessary. As such, Reformer uses Locality-Sensitive Hashing (LSH) to group *similar* tokens together using hash buckets, and after sorting the queries and keys according to their hash numbers, computes sparse attention by only computing attention between tokens in the same bucket. **Note**: *LSH is a broad category of hashing schemes that assigns the same hash to nearby vectors with high probability*. Overall, Reformer is able to reduce the computational complexity from $O(L^2)$ to $O(L * log(L))$. 

BigBird, on the other hand, takes a more generalized approach that induces sparsity by combining 3 different attention patterns: random attention, window attention, and global attention. Random attention enforces all tokens to attend to $r$ random tokens while window attention enforces all tokens to attend to $w$ local neighboring tokens. Lastly, a set of $g$ tokens attend to all parts of the sequence. By carefully choosing the values of hyper-parameters $r$, $w$, and $g$, BigBird showed superior performance against other state-of-the-art transformers at the time. Overall, BigBird reduces the computational complexity to $O(L * (w + r + g))$ where $(w + r + g) \ll L$.

More recently, another sparse attention method designed for graph data called Exphormer [Shirzad, 2023] was introduced with clear influences from BigBird but with robust graph-theoretical motivations. Much like BigBird, Exphormer includes 3 attention patterns. In place of random attention from BigBird, Exphormer uses expander graphs which are sparse graphs with high connectivity with theoretical properties approximating complete graphs. Specifically, Exphormer uses d-regular Ramanujan graphs, which are known to be particularly robust. Next, Exphormer uses neighborhood attention in place of window attention from BigBird by leveraging existing graph topology. Lastly, in place of BigBird’s global attention, Exphormer uses virtual nodes as global connectors to aggregate and propagate information at a global level. Exphormer maintains complexity linear in the size of the graph, or $O(\lvert V\rvert + \lvert E\rvert)$ where $\lvert V\rvert$ and $\lvert E\rvert$ denote the number of nodes (vertices) and edges (links), respectively. In general, attention mechanisms that leverage graph topology for attention calculations such as Graph Attention Networks (GAT [Velickovic, 2018], GATv2 [Brody, 2022]) can be seen as instances of sparse attention and typically carry complexity of $O(\lvert E\rvert)$. 

These sparse attention variants demonstrate the effectiveness of strategically induced sparsity patterns in maintaining representational power while significantly reducing computational demands, allowing for computation of longer contexts and more efficient computation.

### Linear Attention

Linear attention moves beyond the softmax structure and instead views softmax attention as a kernel function to be approximated or replaced. This is done by using what can be intuitively thought of as *reverse kernel tricks* to find high dimensional mapping $\phi(\cdot)$ such that a linear combination $\phi(Q)\phi(K)^T$ establishes an approximation or replacement of the non-linear operation $softmax(QK^T/\sqrt{d_k})$. As such, the full linear attention calculation can be written as $LinearAttention(X) = \phi(Q)\phi(K)^TV$. As a result of de-coupling the softmax operation, linear attention can use the matrix associativity principle to calculate $\phi(K)^TV$ first instead of ever invoking $\phi(Q)\phi(K)^T$, bypassing the quadratic bottleneck entirely. **Note***: it is trivial to show that calculating the $KV$ cache first bypasses the quadratic $L\times L$ bottleneck*. Two seminal works, (1) Performer [Choromanski, 2021] and (2) Transformers are RNNs [Katharopoulos, 2020], have been particularly influential in the research of linear attention methods and are described in detail below.

Performer implements linear attention through a novel approach called FAVOR+ (Fast Attention Via positive Orthogonal Random features). The key innovation in FAVOR+ is the use of positive random features (PRFs) to approximate the softmax kernel in high-dimensional space, which ensures numerical stability in regions where attention scores are close to zero. This technique allows Performer to accurately approximate standard softmax attention while maintaining the computational benefits of linear attention's matrix associativity principle.

Unlike Performer that approximates softmax attention, “Transformers are RNNs” uses deterministic feature maps to replace the softmax kernel altogether, effectively establishing a *new* form of attention mechanism. This deterministic feature map takes the form of $\phi(x) = elu(x) + 1$ where $elu(\cdot)$ denotes the exponential linear unit. This work also establishs a surprising connection between transformers and RNNs. The paper's key insight is that when using causal masking with linearized attention, each new token's representation can be computed incrementally based on previous tokens' representations, similar to how RNNs process sequential data. This recurrent formulation is particularly powerful for autoregressive generation (decoding stage), where the paper demonstrates speed improvements of up to 4000x compared to vanilla transformers while maintaining comparable performance.

### Causal Masking for Sparse and Linear Attention

Causal masking is one component of attention that has not been adequately explored in this section. Because sparse and linear attention explicitly avoid the invocation of the quadratic attention map, causal masking is often a non-trivial task to implement and its invocation varies from method to method. In many cases, application of causal masking can reduce or even break the various optimizations made by sparse and linear attention. In sparse attention, causal masking often requires tracking position indices throughout the sparsification process to maintain proper causality with model-specific considerations. Papers like BigBird and Exphormer do not explicitly detail causal scenarios. In linear attention, causal masking requires the mechanism to either apply causal masking sequentially, effectively turning it into an RNN, or break away from the associativity optimization and turning the mechanism back into standard (quadratic) attention. Therefore, it is common to use sparse or linear attention in encoder blocks of the transformer where causal masking is not required while retaining standard attention in decoder blocks. This approach is particularly effective for tasks where input sequences are significantly longer than output sequences, as is often the case in generative applications. In contrast, sparse and linear attention can be deployed in graph transformers quite seamlessly, as there is typically no causality concerns in graph neural networks.

## Attention Variants for Improving Performance

More recently, there has been a growing interest in modeling attention variants desgined explicitly to improve performance of standard attention, without necessarily changing its structure. I call this class of performance improving attention variants *focal attention*. In a departure from aforementioned attention variants, focal attention aims to improve attention performance, not complexity, by equipping standard attention with specific techniques to improve performance. Focal attention mechanisms represent an innovative approach to enhancing the standard transformer architecture by addressing a fundamental limitation: the tendency of attention to overallocate resources to irrelevant context. These mechanisms preserve the core attention structure while introducing techniques that help models focus on the most relevant tokens and dampen accumulated noise. By enabling more selective processing of information, focal attention variants improve model efficiency and performance across various tasks, particularly those requiring precise information retrieval or long-context understanding. In this article, we explore two recent methods introduced at ICLR 2025, Selective Attention [Leviathan, 2025] and Differential Transformer [Ye, 2025].

Selective Attention introduces a parameter-free modification to standard attention that allows tokens to mask previous tokens deemed irrelevant for future processing. At its core, Selective Attention observes that in standard attention, every token must re-discover for themselves that a past token is potentially irrelevant. To illustrate this point, consider a sequence of tokens $[A, B, C]$. With standard attention, $C$ can attend to both $B$ and $A$, but does not allow $B$ to influence how $C$ interacts with $A$. This means that even if $B$ discovers that $A$ won’t be useful in the future, all future tokens ($C$ in this case) must re-discover this for themselves. This redundancy adds to complexity and noise accumulation. To address this, Selective Attention computes a selection matrix where each token can reduce the attention that future tokens pay to previous tokens. This approach is motivated by the intuition that if a token $i$ pays high attention to token $j$, $i$ has likely absorbed token $j$’s information, which may render further processing of token $j$ unnecessary in the future, leading to token $j$’s masking.  This operation is constrained to be causal and non-negative, ensuring tokens only mask previous content and never boost it. The accumulated masking information is then subtracted from attention logits before applying softmax. This approach enables transformers to effectively *forget* unneeded elements, leading to improved language modeling performance and allowing for substantial reductions in context buffer size during inference without sacrificing quality.

Differential Transformer takes a different approach by introducing a mechanism that amplifies attention to relevant context while canceling noise. It partitions query and key vectors into two groups to compute two separate softmax attention maps, then uses their difference as the final attention scores. This subtraction effectively cancels out common-mode noise, similar to how noise-cancelling headphones work in signal processing. The mechanism is complemented by head-wise normalization to handle the resulting sparser attention patterns. Experimental results show that Differential Transformer requires only about 65% of the model size or training tokens needed by standard transformers to achieve comparable performance, while demonstrating superior capabilities in key information retrieval, hallucination mitigation, and in-context learning.

## Conclusion

In examining the landscape of attention variants, we can see a fascinating convergence between approaches primarily designed for complexity reduction and those focused on performance enhancement. Sparse and linear attention mechanisms, while explicitly targeting computational efficiency, can implicitly improve model performance through their sparsification processes. By forcing models to selectively attend to specific tokens based on relevance criteria—whether through locality-sensitive hashing in Reformer, strategic attention patterns in BigBird, or kernel approximations in Performer—these methods can implicitly filter out noise and emphasize important relationships. Conversely, focal attention variants like Selective Attention and Differential Transformer, though primarily designed to enhance performance through better token selection and noise reduction, implicitly address computational efficiency. By enabling models to effectively "forget" irrelevant information or cancel noise, these approaches reduce the effective context size that must be processed, leading to computational savings during inference. 

This convergence suggests that the distinction between complexity-reducing and performance-enhancing attention variants may be quite fluid and related. The most promising future directions likely lie at this intersection, where mechanisms simultaneously address both computational constraints and attention quality—ultimately advancing toward more efficient, accurate, and capable transformer architectures that can process increasingly complex information with greater precision.
